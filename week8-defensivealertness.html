Session Theme
Defensive alertness and positioning against long direct passes. The conditions in both games force a high defensive line — which creates exactly the exposed space that long direct balls exploit. Players must read the ball early, recover quickly, and hold their shape under pressure.
Why These Conditions
The high-line condition forces teams to push players forward — creating real gaps in behind. The direct-ball condition rewards play that exploits those gaps. Both teams simultaneously experience defending the scenario that has been costing goals. The game design creates the problem; the players must solve it.
The Core Tension
Push your whole team forward to score → leave space in behind → opponent plays direct ball → someone must sprint back. Defending team: read the long ball before it's played, hold position, don't get caught flat. This is the defensive alertness problem in its simplest form.
Conditions — Applied to Both Games
Condition 1 — High Line to Score
All Over Halfway
For a goal to count, the entire attacking team must be in the opposition's half at the moment the ball crosses the goal line. Any player behind the halfway line = no goal. Restart from the GK position.
Forces the team to push all players high — which creates the gap in behind that directly replicates the defensive problem from the last match. The reward (goal counts) only comes with the risk (everyone exposed). Defenders must recognise and anticipate the space they're leaving.
Condition 2 — Direct Ball to Score
Play Into Their Half First
Before scoring, the attacking team must have played at least one pass that lands in the opposition's half. The pass must travel from the defending half into the attacking half — a long ball, a driven pass, or a through ball all count. Short build-up in their own half only = no goal.
Rewards direct play and penalises slow possession. Players must commit to playing the ball in behind — the exact type of pass that has been causing defensive problems. Against a team with all players in their half (Condition 1), this direct ball becomes immediately threatening.
Bonus Condition — Defensive Alertness Point (applied to both games)
If the receiving player has clear space of at least 2m when they receive the direct ball into the opposition's half — no defender within 2m at the moment of receipt — the attacking team earn +1 bonus point regardless of whether a goal is scored. This directly rewards and measures defensive positioning: defenders who track runners and hold tight earn nothing for the attacking team. Defenders who are flat-footed, goal-side, or ball-watching gift the bonus. Coach calls "+1!" live when it occurs.

Coaching Points — Game 1
Defending the direct ball starts before it's played
The defender giving the attacking team the 2m space bonus was not alert to the direct ball before it was played. The moment the opposing team's body shape shows a long pass is coming — the striker drops a shoulder, a midfielder lifts their head — the nearest defender must have already begun adjusting. Cue: "see the pass before it's played."
Alertness
When your whole team pushes — you are the cover
In a 5v5 where all 5 push forward, every player is simultaneously an attacker and the only cover available. The last player to arrive in the opposition's half must already be goal-side of the direct ball runner, not arriving at the same time. Arriving with them is too late — they need a head start.
Positioning
Sprint back — shape later
When the direct ball is played and players are out of position, the first priority is to get back between the ball and the goal — shape can be restored once goal-side. Players who try to find their position first, then sprint back, always arrive too late. Sequence: sprint → goal-side → then adjust shape.
Recovery

What's Different at 6v6
—
Same conditions, but with 6v6 on a larger pitch (~40×28m). The extra player per team means there is more shape to consider — teams can attempt to keep one player deeper while the other 5 push forward. This creates the genuine dilemma: who stays back, and does that forfeit the goal?

The 6v6 Tactical Problem
With 6 players, teams will try to send 5 forward and keep 1 back. But all 6 must cross. This creates a genuine decision: does the deepest player abandon their defensive position to score? And when the direct ball comes, who tracks the runner — the player who just sprinted forward, or the teammate behind them? This is unit defending under real attacking pressure.

Coaching Points — Game 2
Who communicates the direct ball threat?
In 5v5 there's no time for communication — you react. In 6v6 there are enough players that someone can read and call it. The deepest player — the last to cross halfway — has the best view of the opposition's shape. Their job is to call "direct!" when they see the long ball coming, giving teammates a fraction more time to adjust. This is the defensive alertness principle made verbal.
Communication
Win it in 5 seconds — don't recover to a shape first
The 5-second counter-press point rewards the immediate reaction to the direct ball — not the composed defensive recovery. A team that charges at the receiver immediately has a better chance of winning the ball back than one that organises first and presses second. Alertness means action, not adjustment.
Counter-press
Unit shape when all 6 push — the last player's responsibility
With all 6 in the opposition's half, the last player to arrive is the de facto covering defender. They must cross halfway but they don't need to sprint into the box — staying on the halfway line, facing back, watching for the direct ball over the top, is a legitimate and intelligent position. The goal counts, and they are already in the right position to defend.

Pitch Arrangement — Top View
The Server's Two Decisions — Applied Across All Pitches
Option A — Short Pass
Into the Feet
Server plays a grounded pass to an attacker's feet. The attacking team now have an alternative scoring route: complete 6 consecutive passes between themselves without losing possession — worth 1 point. Scoring in the goal is worth 3 points. Shooting is always the higher priority — 6 passes is the consolation route that keeps defenders honest.
+1
6 consecutive passes completed (Pitch A) · 4 passes (Pitches B & C)
Option B — Long Ball
Over the Top
Server throws or plays a high, direct ball over the defensive line — into the space behind the defenders. The attackers must read the ball in flight, get goal-side of the defender, and finish. Defenders who have stepped high to prevent the 6-pass must now recover.
+3
Score in the goal (any route) — goal is always the priority
+1 bonus if defender is caught flat — 2m+ clear
The Defensive Dilemma — Why This Works
The 6-pass reward for the short option forces defenders to make a conscious choice: press high to deny the passes and risk being exposed by the long ball, or sit deep and allow easy passes to accumulate. There is no safe option. Defenders who step high show their positioning — and the server reads that step and plays long immediately. This trains the exact alertness problem: defenders cannot just react to the ball, they must read the server's body position and make an anticipatory decision about whether to hold or press.

Objective
The server decides whether to play short (feet) or long (over the top). The short option offers attackers a 6-pass alternative to shooting — this is the trap. Defenders who recognise the 6-pass route and step up to press it are exactly where the server wants them: high, exposed, vulnerable to the long ball. The practice forces defenders to read the server's intent before the ball is played, hold their position under the 6-pass pressure, and react instantly when the long ball comes.
Sequence of Play
1.
Server starts with the ball at the far end of the pitch. Defender and attacker units set up in their halves. Three defenders hold a compact line — they should be watching the server's body shape, not just waiting.
2A.
Short option: Server passes to an attacker's feet. Attackers try to count 6 consecutive passes (worth 1 pt). Defenders must press and disrupt — but if they step too high they leave space for the long ball and a 3-point goal. If defenders press so high that the space opens, the attacker lays back to the server who plays long immediately.
2B.
Long option: Server plays a high ball over the defenders into space behind. Attackers must time their run to receive on the move and finish. Defenders must have read this and already be dropping — not reacting after the ball is in the air.
3.
The goal is always worth 3 points — the 6-pass route scores only 1. This mirrors real football: protecting the goal is the primary defensive task. Defenders who press too high to stop the pass count gift the more valuable 3-point option.
4.
When possession changes, the server immediately delivers the next ball. Rotating subs come on when coach calls "swap!" — the two subs replace any two players of the coach's choice.

Coaching Points — Pitch A (Your Focus)
Read the server — not the first attacker
Defenders who watch the first attacker receiving the short pass lose sight of the real threat. The server is the decision-maker. When a defender has their head fully turned to the ball in a short sequence, the server reads that and immediately plays long. Defenders should always have peripheral vision back to the server. Cue: "half your attention on the ball — half on the server's hands."
Alertness
Hold the line when the short pass is played — don't all step
When the server plays short, the instinct is for all three defenders to press the ball together. This is the trap. One defender presses; the other two hold the line. If all three step, the space behind opens and one long ball wins the point. The two holding defenders must resist the instinct — they are protecting against the ball that hasn't been played yet.
Unit shape
Count the passes — know when the threat changes
Defenders should count the passes aloud. At 4 passes, the team knows the scoring route is close — the intensity of the press must increase. At 5 passes, all three press to stop the 6th. Before 4, one presses, two hold. This is a tactical decision made on information, not panic. Cue: "call the number — change the press at four."
Decision-making
Long ball: drop the moment you see the server's arm go back
A thrown long ball is telegraphed — the server's throwing arm goes back a full second before the ball is released. That second is enough time to drop two steps and get goal-side. Defenders who start dropping on the visual cue (arm back) will always arrive in time. Defenders who wait for the ball to be in the air will always arrive late. This is the anticipation skill the whole session is building.
Anticipation

Variable Start — Ball from Anywhere on the Goal Line
Each sequence begins with the attacking team. The ball can be placed anywhere along their goal line — left post, centre, right post, or anywhere between. The coach or the attacking GK decides. This means the defending team's pressing shape must adapt to the angle of attack rather than expecting the ball to always come from the same position. Left-side start: tests the defending team's right-side press and cover. Central start: the back-4 pass count is most direct threat — defenders must decide whether to press or hold. Right-side start: tests left-side press, and the right-back is free to carry forward if the defence overloads left.
The Back-4 Pass Trap — Why It Works
The defending team's 2 forwards are incentivised to press the 4 midfielders hard to prevent the 6-pass count. If they succeed — fine. But if both forwards press aggressively, their back 4 are left in a deeper block — and the attacking team's 2 forwards have space ahead of them. The attacking GK can also play long over the pressing line. The tension is identical to the server decision in P2: press the short option and surrender the long-ball threat, or hold and allow the pass count. Six passes earns 1 point — a goal in behind earns 3.
Rules
1.
Ball starts anywhere on the attacking team's goal line. GK or coach places it. Defending team sets their shape before each restart — they are told the ball's starting position.
2.
Pass count: only the 4 attacking midfielders may pass between each other. A pass to or from a forward or the GK resets the count to zero. Players call the count aloud: "one!", "two!" etc.
3.
The back-4 count is live at all times — even while attackers are moving forward. The defending team must track both the count and the forward threat simultaneously.
4.
Defending team high-line rule: all 6 outfield players must be in the attacking team's half when the ball crosses the goal line for a +3 goal. If any player is behind halfway at the moment of scoring, the goal counts only +1.
5.
After possession change: first pass must travel into the defensive half to access full scoring. Ball kept in own half = no pass-count, no back-4 bonus, and any goal scored counts only +1.
6.
Final 5 minutes: remove all conditions — open game. Watch whether high-line habits, direct-ball responses and back-4 awareness carry over without enforcement.
Coaching Points
Defending strikers: press the count or hold the line — decide together
The two defending forwards must make a shared decision on every back-4 sequence: press together, one presses/one holds, or hold both. They must call it before the ball moves. Cue: "call it — both in or both out. Never one of each."
Defensive decision
Attacking midfielders: invite the press — then play over it
The back 4 should be deliberate in making the pass count visible — slow it down, make it easy, draw the press in. Once the defending forwards commit to the press, the pass goes directly to the GK or an attacking forward over the top of the pressing line. The pass count is a decoy, not the primary objective.
Attacking build
Variable start: defending shape before the ball moves
The defending team must set their press shape based on where the ball starts — before it moves. Right-side ball start should trigger a right-tilted press with left-side cover. Central start needs a compact central block. Teams who react to ball movement rather than ball position will always be a step behind. Cue: "look at the ball's position, not the ball's movement."
Alertness
GK as an attacker — use the extra player
The attacking team's GK creates a 7v6 overload. In a 4-2 shape, the GK behind the back 4 is the free player — they can drive forward, take the back-4 pass count at number 7 to break pressure, or play the long ball over pressing strikers. Defenders cannot press the back 4 and mark the advancing GK simultaneously. The GK must be coached to read when to advance.
GK involvement
Progressions
P1
Defending team's strikers may now join the back-4 press at any moment — but if both strikers cross halfway, the attacking team earns an immediate free pass to a striker in behind. This makes the decision to press even more consequential and forces both teams to track the commitment level of the press in real time.
P2
Final 3 minutes: no conditions, no formations — free game. Players choose their own shape and scoring route. Observe whether the session's core habits (reading direct-ball cues, tracking the press, staying alert to the space behind) persist without structure. This is the diagnostic moment of the whole session.
Regression
−
Remove the back-4 pass count for the first 5 minutes — let both teams establish the high-line scoring rule and the direct-ball condition before adding the third layer. Reintroduce the pass count once teams are comfortable with the basic constraints.
P3 — Correct Formations: 7 Attackers vs 6 Defenders
Att: GK + 4 MF (pass count) + 2 FW = 7. Def: 4 DF (high line) + 2 FW (pressing) = 6. All 6 def in att half = +3 goal. GK plays long when def FW press deep into att half.
Variable Start Angles — 3 Key Positions
Three key start positions — each creates a different pressing angle for the defending team. Central start: back-4 count is the main threat. Wide starts: one full-back free, press must tilt to cover.
Watch ForPractice 3 — Full Game
Good Signs
Def strikers making a joint decision to press or hold — not individually
Att back 4 using the pass count as a deliberate invitation to press
GK advancing as an active threat — not static at goal line
Def team adapting press shape based on start position before ball moves
Open final 5 min shows alertness and direct-ball habits without conditions
Fix These
Def strikers splitting — one presses, one holds (no shared decision)
Back-4 count reaching 4-5 without any defensive response — strikers not tracking
GK staying on goal line — remind them of the overload opportunity
Def team reacting to same start angle every time — coach varies deliberately
If Not Working
Three conditions too complex → start with high-line rule only, add back-4 pass count at 8 min mark
Def team never reaching high line for +3 → remind them before each restart what the reward is
Back-4 concept confused → brief freeze, physically show which 4 players count and which don't
