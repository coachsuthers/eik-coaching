The Crossing-Foot Rule
Cone on halfway line · Attacker's direction = dictates finishing foot & goal
← Attacker goes LEFT of cone
Must finish with LEFT foot into the RIGHT goal
Mimics the across-goal shot from the left channel — the most common missed finishing opportunity. Forces the striker to trust their weaker foot and drive across the body.
→ Attacker goes RIGHT of cone
Must finish with RIGHT foot into the LEFT goal
Mimics the across-goal shot from the right channel. The near-post finish (same foot, same side) is not rewarded — only the disciplined across-goal finish counts.
Why this rule matters: Most youth players default to their strong foot regardless of angle, or take the near-post finish because it feels easier. This rule removes that choice and forces the technically correct decision — the one that creates the most scoring opportunity in real match situations, and the one that generates the most dangerous rebound angles for teammates.
Practice 1 — 3v3 Wave Finishing
Wave Format · 3v3 · Both Groups Simultaneous
1v1 Waves — Defender Feeds, Attacker Finishes
Defender passes to attacker · cone decides foot & goal · rebounds live 3 sec · 3 defenders rotate after each set
25MIN
Full Pitch Layout — 4 Quarters (Top View)
Left half (A+B): your 12 players · right half (C+D): weaker group 8 players · goals at top of each pitch · cone on halfway · attacker starts at far end
Objective
Each wave is a 1v1 finishing duel started by the defender. The defender plays a pass to the attacker and immediately moves to defend. The attacker must choose a side of the cone — which locks in both the required foot and the target goal. The defender scores by winning the ball back in the attacker's half. The remaining two attackers watch and rotate in. After all three attackers have gone, the groups swap roles.
⚽ Attacker Scores
+1
Finish with the correct foot into the correct goal
Left of cone = left foot, right goal · right of cone = right foot, left goal
+1
Rebound goal within 3 seconds
Any attacker may follow up — same foot rule applies to the finisher
🛡 Defender Scores
+1
Win the ball back in the attacker's half (before the cone line)
High press earns the point — stopping in defending half earns nothing but prevents the goal
+0
Stop the attacker in the defending half
No point — but prevents the goal. Earns the next wave restart
Pitch A — Crossing-Foot Mechanic Detail
D1 passes to the attacker to start the wave. Attacker drives left of cone → left foot, right goal. Or right of cone → right foot, left goal. Rebound live 3 sec. Defender scores only by winning ball in attacker's half.
One Wave — Step by Step
1.
Defender plays the ball to the waiting attacker from the halfway line. The other two defenders hold their positions on the halfway line. The other two attackers wait behind the attacker's start zone.
2.
Attacker receives and drives toward the cone, choosing to go left or right. That choice immediately determines the required finishing foot and the target goal.
3.
Defender engages once the pass is made — they can win a point by dispossessing the attacker while still in the attacker's half (between the attacker's start line and the halfway cone line).
4.
Once the attacker crosses halfway — the defending zone begins. No point for the defender here, but they still try to prevent the shot. If they stop the attacker in the defending zone, the wave ends with no score for either team.
5.
Shot: rebounds are live for 3 seconds. Any attacker (including those waiting) may follow up a rebound in those 3 seconds — rebound goals count. After 3 seconds or a goal, the wave ends. Next attacker steps up.
6.
Wrong foot = no goal. If the attacker finishes with the wrong foot (near-post side instead of across-goal), the wave ends with no point — even if the ball goes in. Coach calls it live: "wrong foot — no goal."

Coaching Points
Coaching Points
Commit to the direction early — then execute without hesitation
The attacker who drifts toward the cone and then decides is always late. The decision should be made before receiving the pass — read the defending position and choose the side where there's the most space. Arriving at the cone with a decision already made means the first touch takes you there at pace.
Decision speed
Across-goal finish: plant foot level with the ball, drive through the centre
The across-goal finish fails most often because the standing foot lands behind the ball — producing a scooped shot that rises. Plant the non-kicking foot level with (not behind) the ball, lock the ankle, and drive through the centre. The ball should travel low across the goal. Cue: "plant, don't lean."
Technique
Rebound positioning: the two waiting attackers must move on every shot
The 3-second rebound window is wasted if the other two attackers are standing watching. On every shot, both should be moving toward the likely rebound zone — near post, far post, edge of area. Rebounds from across-goal shots most often go to the far side. Train the movement habit now so it's automatic in match conditions.
Rebound intent
Defenders: the pass decides your first step — read the attacker's body, not just the ball
The defender starts the wave by passing — then must immediately read which side of the cone the attacker is driving toward. A defender who watches the ball after their own pass is a step late. The first movement after releasing the pass should be toward the attacker's strong side, cutting the angle before the cone is reached.
Defensive read
Progressions
P1
After 10 minutes: reduce the rebound window to 1 second — only immediate rebounds count. Forces sharper anticipatory movement from waiting attackers and quicker defensive recovery.
P2
Defender can choose to press immediately (to earn +1 in the attacker's half) or drop to protect the goals. This makes the defender's decision explicit — announce before each wave: "pressing" or "dropping." Both must become habits.
P3
Remove the cone — attacker chooses their own side and foot freely. The rule still applies: if you go right, finish right foot, left goal. No cone to guide it — pure decision-making. The technique must now be automatic.
Regression
−
No defender — attacker takes 3 reps per side of the cone at walking pace, focusing only on the foot placement and shot direction. Establishes the body shape before adding defensive pressure. Reintroduce defender after 5 minutes.

P2 — Half-Pitch Setup · Three Stations
Three stations across the half pitch. One cone per station on the halfway line. L activates first, M activates at Stage 2, R at Stage 3. Right attacker rotates to become next defender after each wave.
Objective
Three attackers (left, middle, right) attack one defender in sequence — but each attacker stays live after their turn, so the pressure on the defender builds from 1v1 to 2v1 to 3v1 within a single wave. The crossing-foot rule applies to each attacker's first shot. After that, the pitch is open — rebounds, second balls and combination play are all live. The practice creates the specific scenario the session is designed to train: a shot from one angle creates an opportunity for a teammate already positioned at a different angle.
⚽ Attackers Score
+1
Goal scored with the correct foot on first shot (crossing-foot rule)
+1
Any goal from a rebound or second ball
No foot restriction on rebounds — finish instinctively
+2
Combination goal — attacker assists directly from a rebound or through-ball to a teammate who scores
Rewards the positional awareness the practice is designed to build
🛡 Defender Scores
+1
Win the ball back in the attacker's half during any stage
+2
Win the ball and successfully dribble it back to the halfway line
Rewards the defender who holds shape across all three stages
Escalating Pressure — How Stages Build
Stage 1: L attacks, M reads rebound. Stage 2: M activates, L still live — defender must cover both. Stage 3: R drives, L and M positioned — defender outnumbered. Green dashed = +2 combo pass option.
Wave Structure — Three Stages
1v1
Stage 1 — Left Attacker Activates
Left attacker starts with the ball and attacks the single defender. Crossing-foot rule applies — goes left of centre, finishes left foot into the right side of the goal. Wave continues until the ball is dead (goal, defender wins it, ball out). Left attacker then holds their position in the pitch — they are still active.
2v1
Stage 2 — Middle Attacker Activates
Middle attacker activates with the ball. Left attacker is still live — now a second option for combination play or a positioned target for a rebound. Defender must manage two attackers. Middle attacker: crossing-foot rule on their first shot only. Left attacker free to finish with any foot on follow-up. Stage ends when ball is dead. Both attackers hold position.
3v1
Stage 3 — Right Attacker Activates
Right attacker activates with the ball. All three attackers and the single defender are now live. This is the highest pressure moment for the defender and the richest opportunity for attackers to create rebounds. Right attacker: crossing-foot rule on their first shot. Left and middle free to finish any foot. Wave ends when ball is dead. Full wave complete — rotate.
Rotation After Each Wave
→
The right attacker becomes the next defender after each wave ends. This means every player in the group will defend — and every player who has just experienced the 3v1 as the hardest attacking scenario will know what the defender faces.
→
Left and middle attackers rotate stations each wave: left → middle → right → defender → left. Everyone cycles through all three positions and the defender role.
→
With 12 players and 4 active per wave (3 att + 1 def), the remaining 8 wait in two groups at the side — split by station (left waiters, middle waiters, right waiters). No player waits more than 3 waves.

Coaching Points
Left attacker: your shot starts the rebound chain — aim with intent
The left attacker isn't just trying to score. Their shot should be driven to a zone that creates the most dangerous rebound for the middle attacker who is activating next. Across-goal to the right side of the goal means the rebound typically goes to the middle or far post — directly where the middle attacker should be positioned. Cue: "shoot to score, but know where the rebound goes."
Rebound intent
Middle attacker in Stage 2: you have two jobs before the ball arrives
While the left attacker takes their shot, the middle attacker should already be: (1) reading where the rebound is likely to go, and (2) positioning to be the best second option. If the shot goes in, they immediately think about their own activation. If it rebounds, they're already in the right place. They should never be watching the shot — they should be moving during it.
Anticipation
Right attacker in Stage 3: read the defender's position — not the ball
By Stage 3 the defender has been working for two stages and may be pulled toward one side or out of position. The right attacker should drive to the space the defender is not — the opposite side from where the defender has been drawn. Two teammates are already in the box. The right attacker's job may be to create the assist, not the goal.
Decision-making
Defender: the hardest moment is Stage 2 — hold shape, don't chase
When the middle attacker activates, the instinct is to step toward the new ball. But the left attacker is still live in the box. The defender must hold a position that covers both threats simultaneously — goal-side of both attackers, not fully committed to either. The 3v1 in Stage 3 is less dangerous for the defender who has maintained good positioning than it is for the one who has been pulled all over the pitch.
Defensive shape
Progression — Stage 3 becomes 2v2
+
When Stage 3 feels too easy for attackers: a second defender activates at the same moment as the right attacker — creating a 3v2 (or effectively 2v2 at the point of the right attacker's first contact). The second defender comes from the waiting group. This shifts the emphasis from free finishing to combination play under defensive pressure. Call it when you see the defender being overwhelmed with no defensive resistance remaining.
Regression
−
Pause the practice between stages — allow players to set their positions before the next attacker activates. Slows the wave down but lets players consciously experience and discuss the positioning decisions before full pace is reintroduced.

Practice 3 — Three-Team Rotating Game
Full Game · All 20 · Three Teams · Winner Stays

Teams of 7, 7, 6 · one team rests · rebound across-goal = 2 goals · full quarter pitch

P3 — Three-Team Rotation · Winner Stays
Teams A and B play. Team C rests and watches. A shoots across goal — rebound struck across = ×2. Winner stays. Loser rests, Team C enters. First to 5 goals wins the game.
Objective
A free game that rewards everything the session has built: shooting with intent across goal, generating rebounds, and positioning to attack second balls. Three rotating teams — the winning team stays on, the losing team rests and the resting team enters. The rebound bonus (2 goals for a rebound struck across goal) keeps all players alive and moving after every shot, not just the scorer.
The Rebound Rule — Across-Goal Rebound = 2 Goals
A goal scored from a rebound that is struck across the goal (same rule as P1 and P2 — feet to far side) counts as 2 goals. A rebound tapped in near-post or straight counts as 1 goal. This keeps the across-goal technique alive in a free game context and rewards players who position themselves correctly at the far post.

Rules
Rules
1.
Normal football. No GK. Two teams play, one rests. Winner stays on — losing team rests, resting team enters.
2.
A rebound goal struck across the goal (far post, opposite foot) counts as 2 goals. Coach calls "two!" when it happens.
3.
No crossing-foot requirement on initial shots — players choose freely. The rule only applies to the rebound bonus: to earn 2 goals the rebound must be an across-goal finish.
4.
Game length: first to 5 goals (or 2-goal lead after 4 minutes, whichever comes first). Keeps rotation frequent and intensity high.
5.
Resting team may not advise or coach during the game — they observe and prepare.
6.
If a team wins 3 games in a row, they rotate off and the longest-resting team enters — prevents dominance killing competitive intensity.
Coaching Points
Every shot is a potential assist — move to the rebound before you know if it's needed
The player who moves to the far post before the shot lands will score the 2-goal rebound. The player who watches the shot and reacts after will always arrive a step too late. The habit of moving on every shot — not waiting to see if it goes in — is what this game is training. Cue: "shoot and move, not shoot and watch."
Rebound positioning
Across-goal finishes from rebounds create the best chance — even if harder
A near-post tap-in is 1 goal. An across-goal rebound is 2. In a game to 5, that difference wins games. Players who see that rebounding correctly is the highest-percentage route to winning will start to take the harder finish deliberately, not accidentally — exactly the technical habit from P1 and P2 carried into a competitive context.
Technique under pressure
Observe the resting team — who is talking? Who is watching nothing?
The resting team reveals a lot. Players who are engaged, tracking the game and thinking about what they'll do when they enter are building mental models even without the ball. Players who have switched off the moment they're not playing are losing development time. Note who is watching and who isn't — it's worth a brief observation comment after the session.

Progressions & Regression
Progressions
P1
Increase rebound bonus to 3 goals for an across-goal rebound — teams will explicitly set up to generate rebounds, creating the tactical awareness the session has been building all along.
P2
A goal from a direct across-goal shot (not just a rebound) also earns 2 goals. This ties the entire session together — every across-goal finish is rewarded, whether first shot or rebound.
Regression
−
Remove the rebound bonus entirely — play freely to 5 goals. If the session themes (across-goal finishing, rebound positioning) are still appearing naturally, the habits are embedding. If not, name it in the debrief and plan to revisit in the next session.
