Principle One
Delay
Slow the attack — buy time for the defensive unit to organise. In handball: the front bank holds hands and moves as one, denying forward progress, not breaking early to chase the ball. Breaking hands = the delay has ended prematurely.
Front bank holding shape
2
Principle Two
Cover & Balance
The back bank provides cover behind the front bank and balances the shape laterally — replicating a back four behind a midfield four. When the front bank shifts to track the ball, the back bank must mirror the shift to maintain depth and cover.
Back bank mirroring the front
3
Principle Three
Pressing
A player breaks from their bank to press the ball-carrier — earning their team a chance to win possession. But breaking hands creates a gap in the bank. The press is the highest-risk, highest-reward decision: is the trigger clear enough to justify the gap?
Breaking from the unit = risk vs reward
Why Handball? — How Each Mechanic Directly Mirrors Football

↓ replicates
Defensive compactness — the visual, physical sensation of being connected to your unit. When you feel the gap, you feel the vulnerability.

Practice 1 — Handball Unit Defence
Unit Defending · All Principles
Handball — Two Banks of 4 + Wide Neutrals
⅔ × ¼ Pitch · Delay · Cover & Balance · Pressing · 20 Players
25–30MIN
Objective
Two teams of 10 play handball on a small pitch. Each team defends with two banks of 4 players who hold hands within their bank. Two wide neutral players (one each side) run the full length of the pitch and can be used by whichever team has possession — forcing the defending banks to shift laterally. Attackers score only by header or volley. Defenders score by splitting the attack's chain of passes or forcing a handling error. Players discover — through feeling the gap in their own hands — when and why the unit breaks down.

Rules of Play
1.
Players cannot run with the ball — catch and release immediately.
2.
Ball cannot be thrown above head height when building play. It may only travel above head height as a goal attempt (header or volley).
3.
If the ball hits the floor at any point, possession switches to the other team from that spot.
4.
Goals only count from a header or volley — no catching and touching the goal.
5.
Defending team must start each defensive phase with both banks holding hands within their bank — four players connected, two banks.
6.
Both banks start close together. As play develops they may separate naturally — the front bank tracks the ball, the back bank provides depth.
7.
Wide neutral players can receive from and pass to either team — they are shared. They may move the full length of the pitch at any time.
8.
A defender may break from their bank to press the ball-carrier. This earns a chance to win the ball — but leaves a gap in the bank that is immediately exploitable.
Key Mechanic — The Split Point
If the attacking team completes a pass that travels through a gap in either defensive bank — between two connected players — the coach calls "split!" and notes 1pt to the attacking team. Play continues without stopping. The coach debriefs the split at the next natural break: "where was the gap, whose hands broke apart, and what had the bank been doing in the three passes before it?" This is the core learning moment — players feel the physical consequence of breaking hands before the coach names the defensive principle behind it.
Wide Neutral Mechanic — Forcing Lateral Shift
The two wide players (one on each sideline) are shared by both teams — they always help whoever has the ball. When the attacking team passes to a wide player, the defending bank must shift sideways to account for the new threat angle. This replicates how a football defence must shift compactly to track the ball to the wing. The critical coaching moment: does the whole bank shift together (hands still connected), or do one or two players chase and the others stay — breaking the unit? The wide player forces this decision every time they receive.
Player-Led Questions — Ask During Natural Breaks
When you broke hands to chase the ball — what happened to the space behind you?
After a split point. Let the player who broke hands answer. They will identify the gap themselves.
When the wide player received — did your bank move together or did it stretch?
After a successful wide pass that forced a gap. Ask the bank collectively.
Which is harder — holding hands or knowing when to break?
After ~10 minutes of play. Opens the conversation about delay vs press as a decision.
When does it feel right to break from the bank — what are you waiting for?
Ask the whole group. Guide toward: heavy touch, passed behind, dropped ball — the trigger.
Coaching Points
Hold hands until the trigger — the hands are your shape
While hands are connected, the unit is compact. The moment a player releases hands to press, the unit has an open joint. This should only happen when there is a clear trigger — a dropped ball, a heavy touch, a player turning their back. Premature release = premature gap.
Delay
Back bank mirrors the front — cover is about depth, not proximity
When the front bank shifts right to track the ball, the back bank must shift right too — maintaining the same relative depth behind them. If the back bank stays central while the front shifts, a diagonal pass over the top is open. Coaching cue: "your shadow follows you — so should the back bank."
Cover & Balance
The wide player is your widest threat — shift early, shift together
The moment the ball goes to the wide neutral, the whole bank — not just the nearest player — must slide across. One player sliding while others hold breaks the chain exactly as a through-ball would. Shift as a unit or not at all.
Cover & Balance
Breaking from the bank is a team decision, not a personal one
When a player decides to press, their bank-partner must immediately close the gap left behind — stepping in to hold the chain. This is cover in its most physical form. Before breaking, the pressing player should call it: "pressing!" — so their partner knows to close.
Pressing
Two banks splitting is not a failure — it is cover developing organically
When the front bank pushes up to press and the back bank holds deep, the two banks are replicating a midfield and back four. This organic split is the principle working correctly — the back bank provides depth and cover behind the press. Name it when you see it: "that's your midfield and back four right there."
Cover & Balance
Practice Management — 3 Stages
Stage 1 (first 5 min) — Holding hands only. No breaking allowed. Both banks must hold throughout. This establishes the baseline: players feel what a connected unit is. Ask: "how does it feel when someone pulls away?" Give them the sensation before the decision.

Stage 2 (min 5–20) — Full rules. Breaking allowed. Split points tracked by coach. Wide neutrals active. Let the game produce the problems — don't intervene, just call "split!" when it happens and note it. Debrief at each natural break.

Stage 3 (min 20–25) — Press call required. Before breaking from the bank, player must shout "pressing!" — this forces the decision to be communicated and gives the bank-partner time to cover. Any break without the call gives the opposition a free split point.
Progressions
P1
Remove the holding-hands rule — banks must hold shape without the physical connection. Now it's a decision, not a constraint. Players discover whether they've internalised the principle or relied on the hands.
P2
Introduce a second ball — coach throws it in when play slows. The defending team must reorganise their banks quickly to the new ball position. Replicates defensive transition under urgency.
P3
Allow throws above head height. The back bank must now defend lofted balls as well as ground-level threats — replicating defending crosses and aerial balls into a back four.
Regressions
R1
Reduce to one bank of 4 defending — simplify to a single line defending before introducing depth with two banks.
R2
Remove wide neutrals — attackers confined to central area only. Removes the lateral shift challenge until the front-to-back cover principle is established.

Problems & SolutionsIn-practice fixes — without stopping play where possible
Bank doesn't shift when ball goes to wide neutral — stays central
At the next break: "if the wide player has the ball here — point to where the gap is in your bank." Players identify the open angle. Then: "so what does your bank need to do when the ball goes wide?" Let them answer and demonstrate.
Players breaking from the bank without warning — gaps appear without cover
Call "gap!" live (without stopping) every time a break creates an opening. After the attack: "who heard the gap call? What did you do?" The bank-partner identifies whether they covered. Stage 3 (mandatory "pressing!" call) fixes this structurally.
Both banks rushing up — no depth, attackers easily chip over the top
After the goal or split: "which bank should have stayed back?" Let the back bank players answer. Ask the back bank: "what is your job when the front bank presses?" Guided discovery of the cover role. Do not tell them — let them reach "stay back and cover."
Players treating it as a fun game without defensive intent — not working the principles
This is fine for the first 2–3 minutes. After the first debrief, name the connection explicitly: "holding hands in that bank — that is exactly what your back four should feel like in a football match. When you break hands, you've broken your defensive line." The metaphor lands once they've felt the consequence.
→
Bridge — P1 to P2 (90 seconds, do not skip)
Gather all 20 players. Ask: "When did holding hands actually help you defend — and when did someone break the chain too early?" Take two or three answers. Let players name the moments themselves. Then: "The hands are gone now. Same two banks, same two wide players, same pitch — but this time it's football. You have to keep that shape with your movement and your voice instead of your hands. The principle hasn't changed. Only the tool has." This is the critical pedagogical moment — players own the transfer from the metaphor to the real game.

Objective
Same two banks of 4 defend against 8 attackers and a GK — but now with a football on the floor. The hands are gone; shape must be held through movement, communication and positional awareness alone. The defending team also has a GK behind both banks. Attackers build through both banks to score on the main goal. Defenders earn points by forcing the ball wide and winning possession, then counter-attacking to a small goal on the halfway line. The split-pass point rule carries over directly from P1.

Rules of Play
1.
Normal football rules — attackers build with the ball on the floor to score on the main goal.
2.
Defending team starts in two banks of 4 (or 4+3). No holding hands — shape is held by positional discipline and communication only.
3.
Split-pass rule (from P1): if a pass travels cleanly through the gap between two bank players and a teammate receives it in behind the bank — coach calls "split!" and attackers earn 1pt. Play continues.
4.
Wide neutral players can receive from and pass to either team. They may dribble along their channel but cannot cut inside past the wide channel boundary.
5.
If the ball goes to a wide neutral and the attack subsequently scores from a cross or cut-back — the goal counts double (2pts). This rewards the defence tracking the wide threat and penalises them for ignoring it.
6.
If defenders win possession, they can counter-attack and score in the small cone goal on the halfway line (1pt). They have 6 seconds from winning the ball to reach the halfway goal.
7.
A defender who breaks from their bank to press must call "pressing!" before engaging. No call = attackers receive a free split point.
8.
Teams swap attacking and defending roles every 8 minutes. Scores reset.
Key Change from P1 — How the Split Mechanic Translates
In P1, the physical gap between held hands was the split. In P2, the gap between two bank players' defensive positions is the split — the same principle, now maintained by awareness rather than touch. The coach still calls "split!" when a pass threads the gap cleanly. The debrief question is identical: "where was the gap, who should have closed it, and what caused it to open — pressing too early, wrong shift direction, or one player not tracking the ball?" Players will notice the gap feels different without hands to alert them — this is the discovery moment of P2.
Wide Neutral — Now Able to Cross or Cut Back
In P2 the wide neutral can deliver a cross or cut-back into the box after receiving — not just pass back inside. This creates a new defensive challenge: does the nearest bank player leave their position to close the wide player, or do they hold shape and risk a cross? This is the classic width vs compactness decision that a defensive unit faces in every game. Back bank must now track runs into the box from crosses — replicating what a back four does when a wide player delivers.

· After break-from-unit: "who covered when you pressed?"

· Counter-attack flowing — full sequence plays out

Coaching Points — P2
Shape without hands — voice replaces touch
The hands in P1 told you when someone moved away. Now your voice does that job. Constant communication is not optional — it is the mechanism that holds the unit together. Every time you move, call it. Every time you press, call it. Silent defending is broken defending.
Delay
Front bank delays — back bank reads and mirrors
The front bank's job remains delay — slow the attack, hold the line, force an extra touch. The back bank must read the front bank's movement and mirror it at depth: if the front shifts right, the back shifts right at the same time. The back bank watching their own attacker instead of mirroring the front bank is the most common error in P2.
Cover & Balance
When the ball goes wide — both banks shift, GK communicates
A pass to the wide neutral is the signal for both banks to shift toward that side — narrowing the central lane and covering the near post. The GK is the eyes of the unit: they call "wide left!" or "shift right!" to trigger the movement. If only one bank shifts, a diagonal cross-field pass beats the other bank immediately.
Cover & Balance
Press trigger is a football moment — not a handball moment
In P1 the trigger was the ball hitting the floor or a bad throw. In P2 it is: heavy touch, turned back, pressure from behind the attacker. The pressing player identifies the trigger before breaking — not after. If they break without a trigger, the gap opens for nothing. The "pressing!" call forces the identification before the commitment.
Pressing
Win the ball — transition immediately
When the defending unit wins possession, the counter-attack to the halfway goal must happen within 6 seconds. Defenders who have just won the ball tend to hold it — the habit from defending. Cue: "win it, go!" — the transition must be as fast as the press was disciplined.
Pressing
Player-Led Questions — P2
Without holding hands — how did you know when someone had moved out of position?
Ask after first split in P2. The answer should be "I didn't" — which makes the case for communication without needing to tell them.
When the back bank shifted right — did you check the front bank first or did you just react to the ball?
Ask the back bank collectively. Distinguishes reactive movement from coordinated unit movement.
Compare how it felt to hold the shape in P1 with how it feels now. What's harder — and why?
Mid-practice question. Opens the metacognitive reflection on what the hands were doing for the unit.
When you pressed and won the ball — was the counter-attack ready, or did your team take too long?
After a slow counter. Let the team self-assess the transition speed.
Progressions
P1
Remove the split-pass point rule. Defenders now hold shape purely for defensive reasons — not to avoid a point deduction. This tests whether the principle has been internalised or was only maintained because of the scoring incentive.
P2
Overload attacker. Add a ninth attacker (bring one wide neutral into the central area as a floating attacker). Defending team now faces 9v7 — the unit must hold shape under numerical disadvantage. Replicates a real match where the opposition is dominating possession.
P3
Transition condition. When defenders win the ball, both banks must cross the halfway line together before scoring — replicating a coordinated defensive unit becoming an attacking unit while maintaining shape.
Regressions
R1
Allow players to hold hands again for 2 minutes to re-establish the shape — then remove hands again. Use if the unit completely loses its form when transitioning from P1 to P2.
R2
Reduce attackers to 6 (remove 2) — give the defending banks a numerical advantage to find their shape before going to equal numbers.

The Key Difference from P1 — No Hands
In P1 the holding-hands rule made the unit physical and tangible — players felt the gap when a hand broke away. In P2 that physical signal is gone. Players must now hold the shape purely through scanning, communication and anticipation — exactly as in a real match. This is the step from understanding the principle to applying it. The first 3–4 minutes of P2 will almost always show a deterioration of shape as players adjust. Name this directly: "You've lost the safety net — now you need to trust each other without the hands. That's football."
GK Role — Unit Organiser
The GK is the only player with a full view of both banks. Their job throughout P2 is to call the bank into shape: "back bank drop!", "front bank left!", "gap between 2 and 3!" — constantly. Coach the GK specifically during P2: if they are silent for more than 10 seconds while a threat is developing, call their name and ask "what do you see?" This replicates the GK's organising role in a real match and directly develops the communication habit that was absent in the 6-4 loss.

Coaching Points — Applying the Principles Without Hands
Delay: the front bank holds shape — don't dive in
Without the hands to hold them, the front bank's instinct will be to press immediately. Coach the front bank to set their body shape first — side-on, low, channelling the attacker — before committing to a tackle. The delay buys the back bank time to set their cover positions. A rushed press that fails leaves 8 players in front of the attacker instead of 4.
Delay
Cover: back bank drops when front bank steps up
Every time a front bank player steps up to engage, the back bank player directly behind them must drop half a step deeper and shift to the cover angle. This is the automatic reaction — step up in front, drop back behind. If both banks press simultaneously there is no cover and a single pass eliminates both lines. Coaching cue to the back bank: "if your front player steps up — you step back."
Cover & Balance
Balance: both banks shift together when ball moves wide
When the ball reaches a wide neutral, the entire unit — both banks — must shift across together. The shape must travel with the ball. If only one or two players shift, the gap appears on the weak side. Coaching cue: "the unit is one body — it moves together or it breaks." The wide neutral in P2 is an even bigger threat because they can now dribble as well as pass.
Cover & Balance
Pressing: the "pressing!" call is now the trigger for the back bank
When a front bank player calls "pressing!" and breaks forward, the back bank must hear it as an instruction — not just information. It means: the player to the left or right of the gap must shift across immediately. This is the cover principle in live action. If the back bank is slow to react, the gap stays open and the split is easy. Drill this as a conditioned reflex: "pressing!" call = immediate cover shift from back bank.
Pressing
GK organises the whole unit — not just the goal
The GK is the eyes of the team. They should be calling the bank positions continuously: "front bank — push up!", "back bank — hold!", "gap left of three!" Players in the banks are focused on their immediate opponent; the GK has the picture. This is the most transferable football skill of the whole session — a communicating GK fixes defensive problems before they become goals.
Unit Organisation
Practice Management — 3 Stages
Stage 1 (first 5 min) — Slow build. Attackers play at 70% — no pressing from attackers, just patient possession building. Gives defending banks time to find their positions without hands before facing full pressure. Ask at 3 minutes: "how does it feel without the hands? What's missing?"

Stage 2 (min 5–20) — Full intensity. All rules live. Split points tracked. Both wide neutrals fully active. Coach observes and questions. Maximum two freezes across this phase.

Stage 3 (min 20–25) — Free play. Remove the bank structure entirely — defenders can organise however they choose. Watch whether the two-bank shape persists organically as a preferred structure. This is the evidence of deep learning: they choose the shape because it works, not because it was imposed.
Progressions
P1
Allow lofted balls — wide neutrals can cross. Back bank must now defend crosses as well as ground-level splits. Directly replicates defending in a real match.
P2
Increase to 5v8+GK — add an extra attacker to overload the banks. Forces balance decisions as the unit is numerically stretched. Defenders must identify who is surplus and communicate coverage.
P3
Remove the split-point rule — just play. The defensive structure should now hold without the scoring incentive. Observe whether the principle has been internalised.
Regressions
R1
Reintroduce holding hands for 2 minutes — reset the physical reference point, then remove hands again. Use if shape collapses completely in the first few minutes.
R2
Remove the back bank — defend with one bank of 4 only. Establish the front bank's delay and press before adding the depth of a second bank.

Objective
Three teams of 6 + 2 GKs rotating. Two teams play a 6v6+GK game on a quarter of the 11v11 pitch. The team that concedes a goal rotates off and the waiting team comes on. The team that scores stays on. All three principles remain active but there is no imposed structure — players organise their own defensive shape, choose their own roles, and apply the principles through their own understanding. Coaches observe only, asking questions at rotations.

Rules
1.
Normal football — dribble, pass, shoot. No conditions on how to play or how to defend.
2.
The team that concedes a goal immediately rotates off. The waiting team enters from the halfway line as play restarts.
3.
The team that scored stays on and now defends the opposite goal. The game reverses direction after each goal.
4.
GKs are permanent — they do not rotate with teams. They play for whichever team is defending their goal at any moment.
5.
No coach instruction during play for the first 20 minutes. Coaches ask one question per rotation at the changeover moment — no more.
6.
Players may organise their defensive shape however they choose — no bank structure is imposed. The coach does not correct the shape unless asked.
Why "Losers Off" — The Competitive Design Intention
The rotation mechanic creates genuine competitive stakes without a scoreboard or tournament format. Every defensive moment matters because conceding means your team sits out. This produces the conditions where the principles are tested under real pressure — players who have been standing and watching for one rotation re-enter with urgency and intent. The waiting team is also a coaching opportunity: as they observe, ask "what shape are they defending in? Is it working?" — guided discovery without stopping play.
Player Ownership — Why No Structure Is Imposed
The shift from P2's imposed two-bank structure to P3's free organisation is the most important pedagogical step in the session. If players have internalised the principles, they will organise themselves into something resembling cover and balance — not because you told them to, but because they felt what it does in P1 and P2. If they don't, the debrief reveals exactly where the learning stopped. Either way, the coach learns more by watching than by instructing. The waiting team observing from the side is your best window into whether the principles have stuck.

Rotation Questions — One Per Changeover
To the team rotating off: "you just conceded — what broke down in your shape first?"
Ask as they walk off. 10 seconds, one answer. They identify the failure themselves before sitting and watching.
To the waiting team entering: "you've been watching — what shape are they using to defend? Is it working?"
Ask as they prepare to come on. They've been observing and have an answer ready. Activates the learning from watching.
To the team staying on after scoring: "you scored — what did you do in attack that found the gap?"
Occasionally. Reminds the scoring team that the gap they exploited is the same gap the defending principles are designed to close.
Coaching Points — For the End-of-Session Debrief Only
Did you delay or press immediately? What was the difference?
Ask the group to compare moments where they delayed and moments where they pressed too early. Let them identify the outcomes. The debrief closes the principle — not a lecture, a conversation about what they experienced.
Delay
When someone pressed — did anyone cover the space they left?
The cover question in a free game. Players will recall specific moments. If cover happened naturally without the bank structure, name it: "that was cover — and you did it without being told." This is the most powerful coaching moment of the whole session.
Cover & Balance
Did you organise into a shape? Did it look like the banks from Practice 1?
Connect the three practices explicitly at the debrief. Ask players to describe the shape they used. Most groups will have organically used something resembling a midfield line and a deeper line — they have reinvented the two-bank principle through competitive play. Name it. It was theirs, not yours.
Unit Organisation
End-of-Session Debrief — 5 Min (All 20 Players)
Gather all three teams in a circle. Three questions — ask one at a time, take two or three answers each, then move on:

1. "From the handball to the last game — what changed about how you defended?"
2. "Which of the three principles — delay, cover and balance, pressing — did you find hardest to keep in the real game? Why?"
3. "If you play on Saturday — what is the one thing you'll remember from today?"

Let players own the answers. The coach adds one observation each — focused on a specific player-led moment they witnessed, not a general principle summary.
Progressions
P1
Golden goal — next goal wins the game between those two teams, creating sudden-death pressure. The waiting team stays fresh and is desperate to get on. Maximises defensive intensity.
P2
GK bonus: if the GK organises a defensive action that stops a goal (communicates clearly before the shot), their team earns a free rotation — the losing team stays on for one more minute before rotating. Incentivises GK communication.
Regressions
R1
Reintroduce the split-point rule from P1 and P2 for the first 5 minutes — gives defenders a concrete measurable while they find their shape in the new format.
R2
Reduce to two teams of 8+GK if three-team rotation causes confusion — simplify the format but keep the full pitch and goal. Reintroduce the third team after 5 minutes.
P3 — 3-Team Rotation (6v6+GK · Quarter Pitch · Losers Off)
Team A defends in a player-defined 3-3 (front 3 delay, back 3 cover) — nobody imposed this shape. Team C watches from the side, ready to enter when Team A concedes. GKs are permanent.

Problems & SolutionsPractice 3
No defensive shape emerging — 6 players all pressing the ball simultaneously
Do not stop play. Ask the waiting team: "what shape are they defending in? What would you do differently?" They identify the problem. When they come on, watch immediately whether they implement their own answer.
Competitive rotation causing frustration — losing team visibly disengaged when sitting out
Give the waiting team a specific observational task the moment they sit down — not passive watching. Ask one question as they come off and one as they go on. Active observation removes the frustration of sitting.
Debrief — players can't connect today to what they'd do on Saturday
Ask a concrete question instead of a general one: "on Saturday when they counter-attack — which of the three principles do you use first?" One principle, one answer, one player. Keep it specific. The principle answers itself.
GK not communicating — silent throughout P3
At the next rotation break, speak directly to the GK: "your team can't see what you can see — what do you see right now?" They identify a gap or position. "Tell them." Then let play restart.
