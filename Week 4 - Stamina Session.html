Practice 1 — Line Passing: Turning & Bounce
Technical · Aerobic Base
Line Passing — Turning, Bounce & Switch
4 Groups of 3 · L–M–R Line · 4 Turn Types · Bounce Progression · Speed Race
20MIN
Objective
Four groups of 3 work simultaneously in a straight line — Left (L), Middle (M), Right (R) — with 8–10m between each player. The practice runs in three phases: Phase 1 develops turning technique in the middle; Phase 2 evolves into a bounce-and-switch pattern; Phase 3 adds a timed speed challenge. Every player rotates through the middle position before the group moves to the next turn technique or phase.

Phase 1 — ~18 min
Turn in the Middle
L passes to M. M receives, turns in one touch, passes to R. R passes back — M turns the other way, passes to L. Continuous. After 2 min the middle player rotates out and a new player takes the middle. Repeat the same turn type until all three have been in the middle, then change turn.
↳ 4 turn types · all 3 players in middle each time
Phase 2 — ~6 min
Bounce & Switch
M becomes a bounce player. L passes to M — M lays off first-time. L runs forward past M into the right channel. M steps into L's vacated spot. R receives L's forward run pass, plays back into M. The outside player who received the lay-off has now become the new middle. Continuous rotation — no one stays middle for long.
↳ Outside man switches into middle after lay-off
Phase 3 — Speed Challenge
10 Rotations Race
Using the Phase 2 pattern. One full sequence (L→M lay-off→L switches→R→M→complete) = 1 rotation. All four groups race simultaneously. First group to complete 10 clean rotations wins. If a rotation breaks down, that rep does not count — quality and speed must coexist.
↳ First to 10 clean rotations wins
Phase 1 — Turn Progression (change after all 3 players have been in the middle)
T1
Inside cut — receive on the back foot, drag the ball across your body with the inside of the foot and accelerate away to the opposite side. First touch is the turn.
T2
Outside hook — receive the ball and use the outside of the foot to hook it behind the standing leg, spinning away from pressure. The body shields as you turn.
T3
Cruyff turn — fake to pass or shoot with the instep, instead drag the ball back behind the standing leg with the inside of the foot and rotate 180°.
T4
Drag back — sole of the foot rolls the ball back through the legs, player spins to face the opposite direction. Slowest but most deceptive. Accelerate immediately after.
Rules & Structure
1.
Phase 1: the middle player must complete the turn in one touch — the turn IS the first touch. The pass to the opposite side is the second touch. Two-touch turns do not count and the group restarts.
2.
After 2 minutes in the middle, the coach calls "rotate!" — the middle player moves to one outside, the opposite outside moves to the middle. All groups rotate together on the same call.
3.
Phase 2: the outside player who receives the lay-off does not stay — they move forward into the middle channel immediately, becoming the new middle before the next pass arrives.
4.
Phase 3: one sequence = L plays to M, M lays off to L, L runs into right channel, R plays into L, L passes back to original R, group has switched. That = 1 rotation. Count aloud.
5.
A broken sequence (wrong touch, bad lay-off, player out of position) does not count — the group re-runs that rotation. Quality wins the race, not just speed.

Coaching Points
The turn is the first touch — not a separate action
The most common mistake is to control the ball first, then turn. The whole point of Phase 1 is to make the turn and the control one single movement. If the ball is stopped and then turned, the player has already lost time. Cue: "when the ball touches your foot — you're already facing the other direction."
Phase 1 focus
Show your body — scan before the ball arrives
The best turners scan the space behind them before the pass arrives, so they already know where they're turning to. A player who looks after receiving cannot turn in one touch — they need time to process. Cue: "look over your shoulder before you ask for the ball."
Scanning habit
Phase 2 — the lay-off must open the channel, not just return the ball
The M player's lay-off should be weighted to send L into a forward channel — not a square or backwards pass that kills momentum. A good lay-off means L is already running at pace when they receive it. Cue: "lay it into their run — not to where they are."
Phase 2 focus
Phase 3 — communication accelerates the rotation
The fastest groups will be calling the next pass before it happens. The outside player calling "lay!" as they pass in, the middle calling "go!" as they lay off. Anticipation and communication are the only things that separate groups at the same technical level. Watch for quiet groups — they will almost always be slower.
Phase 3 — speed
Further Progression
+
Phase 3 race with a weak-foot only rule — the lay-off must be played with the non-dominant foot. Adds a technical constraint on top of the speed pressure.
Regression
−
Allow two touches in Phase 1 to establish the pattern before enforcing the one-touch turn. Move to one touch once the body mechanics of each turn are understood.

Connect to the 9 — 4v2 + 9 Under Pressure
9 has Dedicated Defender Behind · Rotate 9 Every 2 Waves · Block the Centre · 12 Players

Objective
One player is the central 9 — the link man between the attacking wave and the goal. A dedicated defender (D-back) marks the 9 from behind throughout the wave, applying constant back pressure. Four attackers must connect through the 9 before shooting. Two outfield defenders hold the central lane and prevent bypasses through the middle. The 9 rotates after every two waves so all players experience receiving under pressure and observe what works from the outside between goes.

The 9 — Link Man Under Back Pressure
The 9 is the link between the attacking wave and the goal — but now a defender is on their back from the moment the ball is played in. The 9 must hold their ground using body position, shield the ball, and lay off immediately to a runner. They cannot shoot, and they cannot hold longer than 2 seconds before the D-back wins it.

Rotating after every 2 waves keeps the role intense and short — each stint is a sharp physical and technical burst. Players watching from outside should be asked: "what is the 9 doing well? What would you do differently?" This observation time is structured learning, not passive rest.
The D-Back — Defender Behind the 9
The D-back starts directly behind the 9 and stays there for the entire wave. Their three jobs: 1. Apply immediate back pressure when the ball arrives — chest to the 9's back. 2. Try to intercept or block the lay-off. 3. Win possession if the 9 takes too long or loses control.

The D-back does not leave the 9 to press attackers or track wide runs. Their only job is the 9. The two outfield defenders handle the wider battle. If the D-back leaves, the 9 gets a free touch — call it immediately.
Defensive Brief — Block the Central Lane
D1
Holds the central lane — positions between the ball and the 9 to cut the passing line before it reaches the 9.
D2
Presses the ball-carrier — forces play wide. Never allows a simple central pass forward unchallenged.
D-Back
Stays on the 9's back throughout — does not track wide, does not leave the 9 for any reason.
If the ball goes wide of both central lane cones: defending team earns 1pt — they have forced play away from danger.

Coaching Points
9: half-turn body shape before the ball arrives
Receiving square gives the D-back equal access to the ball. Half-turned — side-on to the defender — uses the 9's body as a shield and still allows them to see the lay-off options. The body shape is set before the pass arrives, not after. Cue: "shoulder to their chest — feel them, don't face them."
9 technique
9: scan before receiving — know the lay-off before the touch
The best 9s have already decided their lay-off target before the ball arrives. They've scanned, picked the run, and the release is instant. A 9 who looks after receiving will be dispossessed. Link to P1: the same scan-before-touch habit from the middle man in Phase 1.
9 technique
Attackers: curve your run to create an angled lay-off
A runner going straight toward the 9 gets a lay-off that goes backwards. A runner curving around and behind the D-back creates a lay-off into space. The angle of the run determines the quality of the lay-off. Ask: "where does your run need to go so the 9 can play forward, not back?"
Movement
Defenders: hold the central lane — shape before press
D1 and D2 must stay narrow and block the central passing corridor before committing to a press. If they chase wide runs, the central pass to the 9 opens immediately. Their priority is always the central lane first — force the attack wide, then the D-back deals with the 9.

Problems & SolutionsPractice 2
9 immediately dispossessed — can't hold the ball under D-back pressure
Use R1 first. Between waves ask the 9: "which side was the defender on? Which shoulder did you shield with?" Let them answer. The physical diagnosis is more useful than a coaching cue at this point.
Central lane not held — attackers passing straight through easily
At the rotation, ask D1 and D2: "where did that pass go? What were you covering?" Physical demonstration — ask them to show their positions before the next wave. Seeing it beats hearing it.
All runners going the same direction — 9 has only one lay-off option
Ask the watching group: "if you were the 9 right now, where would you want the three runners?" Peer coaching — the observers answer and the active players hear it from teammates.
D-back leaving the 9 to follow the lay-off
Call live: "D-back — back on the 9!" without stopping. After the wave: "what happened when you left the 9?" The gap they created is the self-answer. Name it once and move on.
→

No long explanation — keep intensity up. "6v6. Ball goes out — I play a new one in immediately. Team that concedes sprints to their half before I restart. Central zone condition for the first 15 minutes, then open. Go." The shorter this bridge, the higher the heart rate when P3 starts.

Maximum Intensity · Full Integration

Quarter Pitch · All 12 Players · No Throw-ins · Central Zone Condition · Concede = Sprint
20MIN
Objective
6v6 on the quarter pitch — both goals used. Every natural rest moment is removed: ball out of play means coach immediately plays a new ball in, no throw-ins, no goal kicks. Team that concedes a goal sprints to their own half line before the restart. First 15 minutes: a goal only counts if the scorer received the ball from a teammate in the central zone (maintaining the 9 connection principle). Final 5 minutes: completely open — any goal counts. The Three No-Rest Rules
1. No throw-ins or goal kicks: Ball goes out → coach plays a new ball in from the nearest sideline within 3 seconds. Play restarts immediately from wherever the new ball lands.

2. Goal scored → sprint: The team that concedes immediately sprints to touch their own halfway line before the coach restarts. The scoring team gets a free 5–8 seconds of ball possession while the other team recovers. This is the only built-in rest in the game — and it only comes after conceding.

3. Subs on the move: If the coach needs to bring a player on (injury or rotation), the sub comes on while play continues — no stoppage.
Coaching Points — Debrief Only (Silent for 15 min)
Did you find the central zone when tired — or go direct?
In the debrief, ask players to compare the first 15 minutes (condition on) vs the last 5 minutes (open). Did their play change? Did the central connection habit persist without the rule? Players who found the central zone freely in the last 5 minutes have internalised the principle.
Principle retention
Where was your hardest moment — and how did you get through it?
This session is about stamina. The debrief should name the physical challenge explicitly: "There was a moment today where you were tired and still had to make the right decision. What was it?" Players identify their own resilience moment. This is as important as any technical coaching point.
Mental resilience
Progressions
P1
Reduce to one goal — both teams attack the same goal, defend the same goal (basketball format). Even more chaotic transitions, even higher intensity.
P2
Sprint to halfway applies to the SCORING team too — they must sprint to halfway and back before the other team restarts. Removes the rest advantage of scoring.
Regression
−
Allow 60-second water break at the 10-minute mark — only if players are showing signs of genuine exhaustion rather than competitive fatigue.

Problems & SolutionsPractice 3
Central zone condition slowing the game down instead of raising intensity
The condition should be invisible once understood. If players are hunting the zone too consciously and slowing down to do it, reduce the zone size to just a 5m corridor — forces quicker decision making.
Coach can't keep up with all the restarts — too many balls going out
Have a player act as ball-feeder on each sideline — they collect balls and hand them to the coach. Keeps restart speed up. Name one player per side at the start of P3.
Dominant team scoring frequently — game becomes unbalanced
After three goals in a row by one team, give the other team a free corner or the ball in the central zone. Rebalances without stopping play.
Open final 5 min produces no central passes at all — habit not retained
This is diagnostic — the principle hasn't been internalised yet. Name it in the debrief honestly: "In the last 5 minutes — did anyone still look for the central zone?" Invite reflection rather than correction.
