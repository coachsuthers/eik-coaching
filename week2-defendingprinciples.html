Defensive Principle One
Delay
The first defender's primary job is not to win the ball immediately — it is to slow the attack down, buy time for teammates to recover shape, and deny forward progress. Delay by closing at pace then jockeying, body side-on, forcing the attacker to take an extra touch rather than playing forward immediately.
Foundation of P1 · Applied in P2 & P3
2
Defensive Principle Two
Cover & Balance
Cover: the second defender positions behind and at an angle to the first — ready to take over if the ball-carrier gets past. Balance: the remaining defenders maintain shape across the pitch, protecting lanes the press has vacated. Cover and balance are inseparable — one without the other leaves a gap.
Introduced in P2 · Developed in P3
3
Defensive Principle Three
Pressing
Once the attacker's options are reduced by the delay and the unit is set, the first defender can win the ball. Pressing is triggered by a moment — heavy touch, backwards pass, head down — not by instinct. The delay creates the conditions for the press to be effective.
Earned through delay · Applied in P2 & P3

Edge of Box → Halfway · Individual Defending · Pressing Technique
22 Players · 6 Lanes

Objective
Individual defending in a lane. Each defender starts at the edge of the box; each attacker starts at the halfway line end of the same lane. The attacker tries to dribble the length of the lane to score a point. The defender scores by either stopping the attacker from advancing past them within 10 seconds, or clearing the ball out of the lane. Players rotate after each turn — watching their peers is a deliberate learning tool. A winners' competition is introduced in the progression.

Coaching Points — Delay (P1 Focus)
Close at pace — then slow to a controlled jockey
Sprint to within 2–3 metres of the attacker, then slow immediately. The sprint signals pressure; the jockey is the delay. Arriving out of control means the attacker touches the ball past you — the delay has failed before it began.
Delay
Body shape — side-on, low centre of gravity, weight on toes
Defender's body should be side-on to the attacker — not square. Side-on allows quick movement in any direction. Square means the attacker can go either side equally easily.
Pressing
Choose their direction — the lane boundary is your teammate
Use your body angle to channel the attacker toward the lane boundary. The sideline does the work of a second defender — the delay forces the attacker wide where their options run out. Coaching cue: "show them a direction before they choose their own."
Delay
Delay until the trigger — patience is the discipline
The goal of delay is to make the attacker make a mistake, not to win the ball by effort alone. Wait for the heavy touch, the dropped head, the miscontrol — then commit. Premature lunging ends the delay and gifts the attacker space. Observers: call "too early" or "good moment" after every tackle attempt.
Delay
Progression — Winners Competition
WC
After ~15 minutes: move players between lanes to create the best-of-competition. Each lane nominates its best attacker and best defender based on points scored so far. All nominated attackers compete in one lane; all nominated defenders in another. The winner is the player with the most points across 4 reps. Creates intrinsic motivation, peer comparison, and a higher-intensity final phase to P1.
INT
After the winners' competition: ask the group — "what did the best defenders do differently?" Let players identify the pressing principles themselves before you name them. This is the bridge into P2.
Regressions
R1
Attacker must take at least 3 touches before the defender can engage — gives less confident defenders time to set their body shape before closing.
R2
Widen lanes to ~15m — more space for defenders to set up and reduces the congestion for younger or less experienced players.

Problems & SolutionsPractice 1
Defenders arriving too fast and getting beaten by a single touch past them
Ask the observer: "at what distance should your partner slow down?" Let the observer coach first. If no answer: "show me where you'd start your jockey." Guided discovery via peer coaching.
Attacker always going the same side — defender not showing a direction
Ask the defender after the rep: "where did they go? What does that tell you about your angle?" The defender identifies the problem themselves. Cue: "your body is giving them a free choice."
Observers not engaged — standing passively
Give observers a specific task: "watch the defender's feet — are they on toes or flat?" This makes the observation concrete and gives the observer something to feedback. Peer coaching is an explicit learning tool here.
Winners competition creates frustration for weaker players
Frame it positively: "we're looking for the most improved, not just the best." Or run two competitions: one for the fastest learners and one for the most improved. Keeps all 22 players invested.
→
Bridge — P1 to P2 (90 seconds)
Ask the group: "In the 1v1 — when the defender stopped the attacker without diving in, what were they actually doing?" Let three players answer. Guide toward the word "delaying" if they don't use it. Then: "That delay is what gives your teammates time to get behind you and balance the shape. Now in groups of four, we add the next two principles — cover behind the first defender, and balance so no single pass beats the whole unit. Delay sets everything else up."
Practice 2 — 4v4 Wave Defence
Cover · Balance · Pressing · Unit Defence
4v4 Wave Defence — 4 Central Lanes · Goals Both Ends
Half-to-Box · All Three Principles · Rolling Waves · 22 Players
4v4 Active · 14 Rotating
30MIN
Objective
Outer 2 lanes are removed — 4 central lanes remain across the pitch, creating a focused central corridor. Defending 4 hold the 4 lanes and protect the goal behind them. Attacking 4 try to break through the central lanes to shoot. If the attack goes wide of the outer lane boundaries, the defending team immediately earn 1pt — because wide is where the defence has won. Defenders can also counter-attack by scoring in a small goal on the halfway line to earn 1pt.

Practice Management — 3-Stage Opposition Challenge
Stage 1 (first 5 min): Attackers at 60% — allow defenders time to find the cover and balance positions before full pressure. Use questions at natural breaks: "where should your second defender be?"
Stage 2 (min 5–25): Full intensity. 4v4 at pace. Coach observes and questions — no freezing unless shape completely absent.
Stage 3 (min 25–30): Add condition: if defenders force play wide twice in one wave they earn a bonus point. Focuses the defensive unit on collective shape rather than individual efforts.
Coaching Points — Cover, Balance & Pressing (P2 Focus)
First defender delays — buys time for the unit to set
D1 closes fast then jockeys — not a flat sprint into a tackle. The delay holds the attacker long enough for D2 to find cover and D3/D4 to find balance. A premature press by D1 destroys the whole unit shape before it is set.
Delay
First defender presses on a trigger — after the delay
When D1 closes the ball-carrier, D2 positions themselves 2–3m behind and to the side — ready to take over if D1 is beaten. Cover is not standing next to D1; it is being in the right position to intercept the next move.
Once D1's delay forces a heavy touch or turned back, the press is on. D2 simultaneously steps into cover — behind and to the side of D1, ready to take over if D1 is beaten. Cover is not standing next to D1; it is a position that intercepts the next move.
Cover & Balance
D3 and D4 maintain balance — protect the lanes they don't press
When D1 presses and D2 covers, D3 and D4 must hold their lane positions. They do not follow the ball. Their job is to prevent a single pass beating the whole unit. Ask: "if the ball is played back out — where does it go? Are you there?"
Balance
Press only on a trigger — shape holds until the moment is right
The heavy touch, the turned back, the poor pass — these trigger D1 to press hard and the whole unit to squeeze. Without a trigger, D1 jockeys and the unit holds. Uncoordinated pressing — all four pressing at once — is exactly what creates the gaps the attackers exploit.
Pressing
Wide is a win — force the ball outside the lanes
The 4-lane boundary is the team's best friend in this practice. Showing the attacker outside the central lanes means the defensive shape has worked. Players should celebrate forcing the ball wide — it is the equivalent of a good defensive clearance.
Balance Pressing
Progressions
P1
Add a fifth attacker who starts wide outside the lane boundaries — defenders must now monitor width as well as the central lanes. This forces the balance principle to be actively managed rather than just held.
P2
Defenders must call their role aloud before each wave: one player calls "press!", one calls "cover!", two call "balance!" — forces positional clarity before the attack starts.
Regressions
R1
3v4 — give defenders numerical advantage so the principles can be seen without being overwhelmed. Move to 4v4 once cover and balance positions are consistently found.
R2
Walk-through wave first — coaches position defenders by hand in cover/balance positions, then play is triggered. One demo before live reps. Remove once positions are understood.

Problems & SolutionsPractice 2
All 4 defenders chase the ball — no cover or balance maintained
At next natural break: "if all four of you are here — point to where a pass goes." Let the group identify the four empty lanes. Then ask D2: "where should you have been?" Guided discovery; they position themselves from their own answer.
D2 standing next to D1 rather than behind at cover angle
Ask D1: "if I beat you now, can D2 get the ball?" If D2 is alongside, the answer is no. Ask D2 to take two steps back and to the side — physically show the cover angle. Then play continues immediately.
Attacking team easily passing between lanes — balance is not held
Ask the watching group: "which lane was empty?" The observers identify it. Ask the defender responsible: "where were you when that pass happened?" — not a criticism, an analytical question.
Defenders press before a trigger — immediately beaten by a touch past them
After the rep: "what was the trigger? Was there one?" Let the defender and their teammates answer. The concept of pressing with a trigger versus pressing instinctively becomes a player-owned principle.
→
Bridge — P2 to P3 (60 seconds)
Ask: "In the wave game — which role was hardest: pressing, covering or balancing? Why?" Take two or three answers. Then: "In the full game now, all three happen at the same time across the whole pitch. You won't have lane lines telling you where to stand — you'll need to read the game and find your role. Same three words: press, cover, balance. Call them as you play." This sets the expectation for self-organisation in P3 and plants the verbal communication habit before the game starts.

Coaching Points — For Questions at Natural Breaks
"Before you pressed — did you delay first?"
Ask after a defender charges straight into a tackle that fails. The distinction between delay (jockey, buy time, force the touch) and press (commit to win the ball) is the key diagnostic. If the player went straight to press without delay the unit had no time to set.
Delay
"When you pressed just then — was there a trigger?"
Ask the pressing player after a press that was beaten. Not accusatory — diagnostic. The goal is for the player to self-identify whether they pressed with a reason or by instinct.
Pressing
"Where was the cover when the ball went through?"
Ask after a central penetration. The question identifies whether D2 had found cover position or had drifted. If the player cannot answer, ask their nearest teammate — peer accountability without blame.
Cover
"When D1 pressed — what did D3 and D4 do?"
The balance question. Did the rest of the unit hold shape or chase? This is the most common failure in a full game — pressing triggers collective drift. The question surfaces the principle without lecturing.
Balance
Progressions
P1
GK must call at least one organisational instruction per defensive phase ("cover left!", "press now!") — develops GK as unit organiser. One missed call = opposition free-kick from halfway.
Regressions
R1
Reduce to 8v8 on a smaller pitch if 11v11 is generating too much chaos for the principles to be identified. Larger numbers can dilute individual responsibility for cover and balance.
End-of-Session Debrief — 5 min (Do Not Skip)
Gather all 22 players in a circle. Go around and ask each player: "Name one moment you used press, cover or balance today — and what happened?" This is the pedagogical close of the session. Players articulating principles in their own words is the evidence of learning. Coaches offer one positive observation each — focused on communication and decision-making, not results.
